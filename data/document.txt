I’ve been a data scientist for 6 years now. If you’d asked me what I’m an expert in, I would have confidently said “classification models for tabular data”.

But here’s the thing about thinking you’re an expert: that’s usually when you discover how much more there is to learn.

A few weeks ago, while working on a project that depended on probability prediction, I had one of those “Wait, have I been doing this wrong the whole time?” moments.

But let’s start at the beginning.

Step one: What really matters?
To choose the right metric, you first have to figure out what really matters for your problem.

In my case, I was working on a model whose outputs directly influenced economic value calculations. So it wasn’t just about getting the classes right, it was about getting the probabilities right.

A model that is able to estimate good probabilities is called a calibrated model.

In practice, a model is calibrated if, for all the observations it assigns a, let’s say, 25% probability of being positive, roughly 25% of them actually are positive.

So, what metrics should I use to make sure that my model is calibrated?

What are my benchmark metrics?
Recently, I’ve read many posts on LinkedIn about calibration. The authors of these posts (for example, here and here) claim that looking at the ROC-AUC score alone is not enough. In fact, to make sure that the model is well calibrated, you should use Brier score and/or Log-loss.

So, I decided to track all of these metrics:

1. ROC-AUC score

Measures how well the model ranks positives above negatives. It says nothing about the quality of predicted probabilities.

2. Brier score

If you’re familiar with regression models, the Brier score is simply the mean squared error of the predicted probabilities, in other words:

brier_score = np.mean((y_proba - y_true) ** 2)
3. Log-loss

Measures the likelihood of predicted probabilities given the true labels:

log_loss = - np.mean(y_true * np.log(y_proba) + (1 - y_true) * np.log(1 - y_proba))
Since I can’t share the real data I’m working on, in this post I’ll have to use a public dataset, the “Bank” dataset from PyCaret. That’s not a problem, though: the insights are the same as those I uncovered in the original project.

Here’s what I did to get the results I’ll discuss in this post:

I randomly extracted 100 different subsets of predictive variables, simulating a crude form of feature selection.
I trained 4 models on each subset of variables: Logistic Regression, Decision Tree, Random Forest, and LightGBM.
That gave me 100 × 4 = 400 models.
I tracked all metrics on a holdout test set.

The calibration plot
The most intuitive way to visualize the calibration of a model is the “calibration plot”.

Here’s the idea: you split predicted probabilities into bins. Then, for each bin, you compare the average predicted probability (x-axis) to the actual fraction of positives (y-axis).

So I’ll take one model at random (from my 400 candidate models) and draw its calibration plot. Underneath the calibration plot, I will also add a histogram of the predicted probabilities.

The interpretation of the calibration plot is straightforward. If the model is perfectly calibrated, the curve should lie exactly on the 45-degree line. For example, if a bin has an average predicted probability of 25%, then 25% of the actual outcomes in that bin should be positive. If that was true for each bin, then the curve would coincide with the 45-degree line.

So, the farther the single points of the calibration curve are from that diagonal, the more mis-calibrated the model is.

A metric for calibration: Expected Calibration Error (ECE)
Everybody likes plots. But besides the calibration plot, we need a quantitative way to measure (mis)calibration. There is a metric that stems directly from the calibration plot and is called Expected Calibration Error (ECE).

Conceptually, ECE summarizes how far the points on the calibration curve are from the 45-degree line.

So, imagine we have the following quantities:

proba_means: a vector containing the average predicted probability for each bin.
y_means: a vector with the fraction of positives for each bin.
counts: a vector with the count of individuals in each bin.
Then ECE is simply the absolute distance of each bin’s average predicted probability from the fraction of positives, weighted by the size of the bin.

ece = np.sum(np.abs((proba_means - y_means) * counts)) / np.sum(counts)
I like ECE because it measures calibration explicitly. That’s not quite true for metrics like Brier score or Log-loss, which measure calibration with other things.

ECE is also very intuitive. No logs, no squares, just average absolute errors.

So I thought, since the main thing I care about is calibration, why don’t I just use ECE to evaluate my model, instead of looking also at the other metrics?

ECE alone is not enough
I computed the ECE for all 400 models. Then I sorted them and found out that the model with the lowest ECE had an ECE of just 0.21%. Amazing, right?

Out of curiosity, I looked also at the other metrics. ROC-AUC: 54%, which means it is barely better than random guessing.

How is this possible?

How can a model be super well-calibrated (an ECE of just 0.21%), but be so bad at ranking (ROC-AUC equal to 54%)?
